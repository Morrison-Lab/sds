Exponential Families

Last modified: 2026-11-10 22:42:12 (PST)

Many of the distributions in this course, such as the Bernoulli, Poisson and Normal distributions, share one algebraic form for their probability mass function or density. Distributions with that form are called an exponential family, and they share properties that make estimation easier. This page defines the form and its natural parameter, and checks the form for the Poisson distribution on data.

1 The exponential family form

Definition 1 (Exponential family) Let \(X\) be a random variable whose distribution depends on one parameter \(\eta\), and let \(\mathbb{f}\mathopen{}\left(x; \eta\right)\mathclose{}\) be its probability mass function or density. The distributions of \(X\) form an exponential family if there are a nonnegative function \(h\) and a function \(T\) of \(x\) alone, and a function \(A\) of \(\eta\) alone, such that

\[ \mathbb{f}\mathopen{}\left(x; \eta\right)\mathclose{} = {{h\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\, {T\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}} - {A\mathopen{}\left(\eta\right)\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} \tag{1}\]

for every value \(x\) and every allowed \(\eta\).

Remark 1 (Source: Exponential family). Casella and Berger [1, Sec. 3.4], Eq. 3.4.1 write the family as a sum of \(k\) terms inside the exponential, each a function of the parameter times a function of \(x\); Dobson and Barnett [2, Sec. 3.2], Eq. 3.3 write it in the form \({\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{a(y) b(\theta) + c(\theta) + d(y)\right\}\mathclose{}}\). Equation 1 is the case of one term. It is Dobson and Barnett’s canonical form when \({T\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}} = x\), and the natural parameter \(\eta\) plays the role of their \(b(\theta)\). See the notation choices for how the symbols line up.

Example 1 (The Poisson distribution is an exponential family) Let \(X\sim \operatorname{Pois}({\lambda})\), the Poisson distribution with rate \({\lambda}> 0\). Its probability mass function has one factor free of \({\lambda}\), and the factors \({\lambda}^{x}\) and \({\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-{\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}}\). We rewrite it in the form of Equation 1 by writing \({\lambda}^{x}\) as an exponential of a logarithm:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{f}\mathopen{}\left(x; {\lambda}\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{{\lambda}^{x} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-{\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}}}{x!} && \text{(Poisson mass function)}\\ &= \frac{1}{x!} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\lambda}^{x}\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-{\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(a positive number is the exponential of its logarithm)}\\ &= \frac{1}{x!} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x\, {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-{\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(the logarithm of a power is the exponent times the logarithm)}\\ &= \frac{1}{x!} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x\, {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}} - {\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(the product of exponentials is the exponential of the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

So the Poisson distributions are an exponential family with \({{h\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}}} = 1 / x!\), \({T\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}} = x\), natural parameter \(\eta= {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}}\), and \({A\mathopen{}\left(\eta\right)\mathclose{}} = {\lambda}= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\right\}\mathclose{}}\).

To check the algebra on data, we use the number of tropical cyclones in northeastern Australia in each of 13 seasons [2, Sec. 1.6.5], with the rate set to the sample mean. We evaluate both sides of Equation 1 at every observed count.

  • R
  • Python
cyclones <- c(6, 5, 4, 6, 6, 3, 12, 7, 4, 2, 6, 7, 4)
rate <- mean(cyclones)

base_measure <- function(x) 1 / factorial(x)
natural_parameter <- log(rate)
log_partition <- function(eta) exp(eta)

exponential_form <- base_measure(cyclones) *
  exp(natural_parameter * cyclones - log_partition(natural_parameter))

data.frame(
  count = cyclones,
  poisson = dpois(cyclones, rate),
  exponential_form = exponential_form
)
max(abs(dpois(cyclones, rate) - exponential_form))
#> [1] 1.11022e-16
import numpy as np

Definition 2 (Natural parameter) In the form of Equation 1 of an exponential family, the parameter \(\eta\) that multiplies the statistic \({T\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}}\) inside the exponential is the natural parameter, also called the canonical parameter [2, Sec. 3.2].

Example 2 (The natural parameter of the Poisson distribution is the log rate) In Example 1, the Poisson rate \({\lambda}\) and the natural parameter \(\eta\) are related by

\[ \eta= {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\lambda}\right\}\mathclose{}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad {\lambda}= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\right\}\mathclose{}} \]

For the cyclone data, the rate set to the sample mean is 5.538 cyclones per season, so the natural parameter is \(\eta= 1.712\). The natural parameter can be any real number, while the rate must be greater than zero.

Example 3 (The Bernoulli distribution is an exponential family)  

Let \(X\sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(\pi)\), the Bernoulli distribution with success probability \(\pi\in (0, 1)\). Its probability mass function is \(\pi^{x} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \pi\right)\mathclose{}^{1 - x}\) for \(x\in {\mathopen{}\left\{0, 1\right\}\mathclose{}}\). We rewrite it in the form of Equation 1 by collecting the factors that have the exponent \(x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{f}\mathopen{}\left(x; \pi\right)\mathclose{} &= \pi^{x} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \pi\right)\mathclose{}^{1 - x} && \text{(Bernoulli mass function)}\\ &= \pi^{x} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \pi\right)\mathclose{}^{-x} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \pi\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(a power with a sum of exponents is the product of the powers)}\\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}\right)\mathclose{}^{x} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \pi\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(powers with the same exponent combine)}\\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}\right)\mathclose{}^{x}\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \pi\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(a positive number is the exponential of its logarithm)}\\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x\, {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \pi\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(the logarithm of a power is the exponent times the logarithm)}\\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x\, {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{1 - \pi\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(a positive number is the exponential of its logarithm)}\\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x\, {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}\right\}\mathclose{}} + {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{1 - \pi\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(the product of exponentials is the exponential of the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

The statistic is \({T\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}} = x\), and \({{h\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}}} = 1\). The natural parameter is the log odds, \(\eta= {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\pi/ \mathopen{}\left(1 - \pi\right)\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}}\). Inverting it gives \(\pi= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\right\}\mathclose{}} / \mathopen{}\left(1 + {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\right\}\mathclose{}}\right)\mathclose{}\), so \(1 - \pi= 1 / \mathopen{}\left(1 + {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\right\}\mathclose{}}\right)\mathclose{}\), and the last term in the exponent is \({\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{1 - \pi\right\}\mathclose{}} = -{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{1 + {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}}\). Hence the Bernoulli distributions are an exponential family with \({A\mathopen{}\left(\eta\right)\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{1 + {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}}\).

To check the algebra on data, we use the birthwt data, which record the birth weight of babies born at Baystate Medical Center in Springfield, Massachusetts [3], as distributed in R’s MASS package [4]. The variable low is 1 for a baby with low birth weight and 0 otherwise. We set the success probability to the sample mean of low, and evaluate both sides of Equation 1 at the two possible values of \(x\).

  • R
  • Python
low <- MASS::birthwt$low
prob <- mean(low)

natural_parameter <- log(prob / (1 - prob))
exp_natural_parameter <- exp(natural_parameter)
log_partition <- log(1 + exp_natural_parameter)

x <- c(0, 1)
exponent <- natural_parameter * x - log_partition
exponential_form <- exp(exponent)
bernoulli <- dbinom(x, size = 1, prob = prob)

abs_difference <- abs(bernoulli - exponential_form)
max_difference <- max(abs_difference)

tibble::tibble(x, bernoulli, exponential_form)
max_difference
#> [1] 1.11022e-16
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import bernoulli

birthwt = pd.read_csv("data/birthwt.csv")
prob = birthwt["low"].mean()

natural_parameter = np.log(prob / (1 - prob))
log_partition = np.log(1 + np.exp(natural_parameter))

x = np.array([0, 1])
exponential_form = np.exp(natural_parameter * x - log_partition)

table = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "x": x,
        "bernoulli": bernoulli.pmf(x, prob),
        "exponential_form": exponential_form,
    }
)
print(table)
#>    x  bernoulli  exponential_form
#> 0  0   0.687831          0.687831
#> 1  1   0.312169          0.312169
print(np.max(np.abs(bernoulli.pmf(x, prob) - exponential_form)))
#> 2.220446049250313e-16

The value of prob is 0.312, so the natural parameter is \(\eta= -0.79\). The two columns agree to within rounding error: the largest difference is 1.1e-16.

Example 4 (The Gaussian distribution with known variance is an exponential family)  

Let \(X\sim \operatorname{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), the Gaussian distribution with mean \(\mu\in \mathbb{R}\) and a variance \(\sigma^2> 0\) that we treat as known. Its density is \(\mathopen{}\left(2 \pi \sigma^2\right)\mathclose{}^{-1/2} {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\mathopen{}\left(x- \mu\right)\mathclose{}^2 / \mathopen{}\left(2 \sigma^2\right)\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}}\). We rewrite it in the form of Equation 1 by expanding the square in the exponent and separating the factors that involve \(\mu\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{f}\mathopen{}\left(x; \mu\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{\mathopen{}\left(x- \mu\right)\mathclose{}^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(Gaussian density)}\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{x^2 - 2 x\mu+ \mu^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(the square of a difference)}\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{x^2}{2 \sigma^2} + \frac{x\mu}{\sigma^2} - \frac{\mu^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(divide each term by the denominator)}\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{x^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} x- \frac{\mu^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(the exponential of a sum is the product of the exponentials)} \end{aligned} \]

The statistic is \({T\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}} = x\), and \({{h\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}}} = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-x^2 / \mathopen{}\left(2 \sigma^2\right)\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}} / \sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}\), which does not involve \(\mu\). The natural parameter is \(\eta= \mu/ \sigma^2\). Inverting it gives \(\mu= \sigma^2\eta\), so \(\mu^2 / \mathopen{}\left(2 \sigma^2\right)\mathclose{} = \sigma^2\eta^2 / 2\). Hence the Gaussian distributions with a known variance are an exponential family with \({A\mathopen{}\left(\eta\right)\mathclose{}} = \sigma^2\eta^2 / 2\).

To check the algebra on data, we use the birth weights in grams from the birthwt data [3], as distributed in R’s MASS package [4]. We set \(\mu\) to the sample mean and \(\sigma^2\) to the sample variance of the birth weights, and evaluate both sides of Equation 1 at every observed birth weight.

  • R
  • Python
bwt <- MASS::birthwt$bwt
mean_bwt <- mean(bwt)
sd_bwt <- sd(bwt)
variance <- sd_bwt^2

natural_parameter <- mean_bwt / variance
log_partition <- variance * natural_parameter^2 / 2

base_exponent <- -bwt^2 / (2 * variance)
base_scale <- sqrt(2 * pi * variance)
base_measure <- exp(base_exponent) / base_scale

exponent <- natural_parameter * bwt - log_partition
exponential_form <- base_measure * exp(exponent)
normal <- dnorm(bwt, mean = mean_bwt, sd = sd_bwt)

relative_difference <- abs(normal - exponential_form) / normal
max_relative_difference <- max(relative_difference)

tibble::tibble(bwt, normal, exponential_form)
max_relative_difference
#> [1] 3.52031e-15
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import norm

birthwt = pd.read_csv("data/birthwt.csv")
bwt = birthwt["bwt"]
mean_bwt = bwt.mean()
variance = bwt.var(ddof=1)

natural_parameter = mean_bwt / variance
log_partition = variance * natural_parameter**2 / 2

base_measure = np.exp(-bwt**2 / (2 * variance)) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * variance)
exponential_form = base_measure * np.exp(natural_parameter * bwt - log_partition)
normal = norm.pdf(bwt, loc=mean_bwt, scale=np.sqrt(variance))

table = pd.DataFrame(
    {"bwt": bwt, "normal": normal, "exponential_form": exponential_form}
)
print(table.head())
#>     bwt    normal  exponential_form
#> 0  2523  0.000463          0.000463
#> 1  2551  0.000473          0.000473
#> 2  2557  0.000475          0.000475
#> 3  2594  0.000487          0.000487
#> 4  2600  0.000489          0.000489
print(np.max(np.abs(normal - exponential_form) / normal))
#> 3.324739754359985e-15

For these data, \(\eta= 0.00554\) per gram. The two columns agree to within rounding error: the largest relative difference is 3.5e-15.

Example 5 (The uniform distribution on \([0, \beta]\) is not an exponential family)  

Let \(X\sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0, \beta)\), the uniform distribution on the interval from \(0\) to \(\beta> 0\). Its density is

\[ \mathbb{f}\mathopen{}\left(x; \beta\right)\mathclose{} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\beta}, & 0 \le x\le \beta\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Suppose these densities were those of an exponential family, indexed by some parameter \(\eta\) that determines \(\beta\). Then Equation 1 would give

\[ \mathbb{f}\mathopen{}\left(x; \eta\right)\mathclose{} = {{h\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}}} \, {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta\, {T\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}} - {A\mathopen{}\left(\eta\right)\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}}. \]

The exponential function is positive for every \(\eta\) and every \(x\), so \(\mathbb{f}\mathopen{}\left(x; \eta\right)\mathclose{} > 0\) exactly when \({{h\mathopen{}\left(x\right)\mathclose{}}} > 0\). The set of values \(x\) with a positive density would then be the same for every parameter value.

The uniform density does depend on it. At \(x= 3/2\), the density is \(1/2\) when \(\beta= 2\) and \(0\) when \(\beta= 1\). So the uniform densities on \([0, \beta]\) are not an exponential family. The set of values \(x\) with a positive density depends on the parameter, which the form of Equation 1 does not allow.
