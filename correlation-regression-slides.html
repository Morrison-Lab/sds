Correlation and Simple Linear Regression

Last modified: 2026-11-04 10:46:13 (PST)

This page reviews two ways to relate two continuous variables: correlation coefficients, with tests of whether they differ from zero, and simple linear regression. It uses the \(t\) reference distribution defined on the Statistical Inference page. This page is adapted from Vittinghoff et al. [1], Chapter 3.

2 Correlation

2.1 Testing the Pearson correlation

Definition 1 (Population correlation) The population correlation of two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with positive variances is

\[\rho\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{{\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X, Y\right)\mathclose{}}}{\sqrt{{\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(X\right)\mathclose{}} {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y\right)\mathclose{}}}},\]

where \({\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X, Y\right)\mathclose{}}\) is their covariance.

Source: Wikipedia contributors [2].

Remark 1 (The sample correlation estimates the population correlation). The Pearson correlation coefficient \(r\) of a sample is the corresponding sample statistic, and estimates \(\rho\).

Definition 2 (Test of zero correlation) Let \(r\) be the Pearson correlation coefficient of \(n \ge 3\) pairs, with \(\mathopen{}\left|r\right|\mathclose{} < 1\). The t-test of zero correlation of \(H_0: \rho= 0\) against \(H_1: \rho\neq 0\) (Definition 1) uses the statistic

\[t \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{r \sqrt{n - 2}}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}}.\]

Its p-value is \(\Pr(\mathopen{}\left|T\right|\mathclose{} \ge \mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{})\), where \(T\) has the \(t_{n-2}\) distribution (t-distribution).

Theorem 1 (Null distribution of the correlation t statistic) Let the pairs \((X_1, Y_1), \ldots, (X_n, Y_n)\) be independent, and let each \(Y_i\), given \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\), be Gaussian with a mean and variance that do not depend on the \(X\) values. Then the statistic \(t\) of Definition 2 has the \(t_{n-2}\) distribution [3, Sec. 9.6], pp. 472-473:

\[ t \sim t_{n-2}. \]

Remark 2 (When the conditions hold). The conditions of Theorem 1 hold, for example, when the pairs are independent draws from a bivariate Gaussian distribution with \(\rho= 0\).

2.2 Spearman rank correlation

Definition 3 (Spearman rank correlation) Replace each \(x_i\) by its rank \(u_i\) among \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\), and each \(y_i\) by its rank \(v_i\) among \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\), giving tied values the average of the ranks they span. The Spearman rank correlation \(r_S\) is the Pearson correlation coefficient evaluated at the rank pairs:

\[ r_S \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}r\mathopen{}\left((u_1, v_1), \ldots, (u_n, v_n)\right)\mathclose{}. \]

Source: Wikipedia contributors [5].

Remark 3 (What the Spearman correlation measures). Because ranks depend only on the ordering of the values, \(r_S\) measures how close the association is to monotone, whether or not it is linear, and a single extreme value moves \(r_S\) less than it moves \(r\).

Example 2 (Spearman correlation between BMI and fasting glucose in HERS) The Pearson correlation of the ranks (Definition 3), and cor.test()’s Spearman test (exact = FALSE, because tied values rule out the exact p-value):

hers_bmi <- hers |> dplyr::filter(!is.na(BMI))
cor(rank(hers_bmi$BMI), rank(hers_bmi$glucose))
#> [1] 0.333751
cor.test(hers_bmi$BMI, hers_bmi$glucose, method = "spearman", exact = FALSE)
#> 
#>  Spearman's rank correlation rho
#> 
#> data:  hers_bmi$BMI and hers_bmi$glucose
#> S = 2.33e+09, p-value <2e-16
#> alternative hypothesis: true rho is not equal to 0
#> sample estimates:
#>      rho 
#> 0.333751

\(r_S\) is larger than the Pearson \(r\) of Example 1: the association is closer to monotone than to linear.

3 Simple linear regression

3.1 Model specification

Definition 4 (Conditional Gaussian model) A conditional Gaussian model for outcomes \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\) with covariate values \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) (each a single value or a vector) says that, given the covariates, the outcomes are independent and Gaussian: outcome \(Y_i\) is centered on a mean function \(\mu(x)\) evaluated at \(x_i\), and has its own variance \(\sigma^2_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_i\mid X_i= x_i&\ \sim_{\perp\!\!\!\perp}\ {\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}},\\ \mu_i&\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mu(x_i). \end{aligned} \]

Definition 5 (Deviation from the conditional mean) In a conditional Gaussian model (Definition 4), the deviation \(\mathbb{d}_i\) of outcome \(Y_i\) is its deviation from its population mean, taking the mean conditional on \(X_i= x_i\):

\[\mathbb{d}_i\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}Y_i- \mu(x_i).\]

Exercise 1 (Decomposing an outcome) Express each outcome \(Y_i\) in a conditional Gaussian model (Definition 4) in terms of its conditional mean \(\mu_i\) and its deviation \(\mathbb{d}_i\) (Definition 5).

Solution 1. Check that \(Y_i= \mu_i+ \mathbb{d}_i\), starting from the right-hand side:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_i+ \mathbb{d}_i &= \mu(x_i) + \mathbb{d}_i && \text{(definition of $\mu_i$)}\\ &= \mu(x_i) + \mathopen{}\left(Y_i- \mu(x_i)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of $\mathbb{d}_i$)}\\ &= \mu(x_i) + Y_i- \mu(x_i) && \text{(remove the parentheses)}\\ &= Y_i+ \mu(x_i) - \mu(x_i) && \text{(reorder the terms)}\\ &= Y_i+ \mathopen{}\left(\mu(x_i) - \mu(x_i)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(group the last two terms)}\\ &= Y_i+ 0 && \text{($a - a = 0$)}\\ &= Y_i && \text{($a + 0 = a$)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(Y_i= \mu_i+ \mathbb{d}_i\).

Alternatively, solve the definition of \(\mathbb{d}_i\) for \(Y_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{d}_i &= Y_i- \mu(x_i) && \text{(definition of $\mathbb{d}_i$)}\\ \mathbb{d}_i+ \mu(x_i) &= Y_i- \mu(x_i) + \mu(x_i) && \text{(add $\mu(x_i)$ to both sides)}\\ \mathbb{d}_i+ \mu(x_i) &= Y_i+ \mathopen{}\left(-\mu(x_i)\right)\mathclose{} + \mu(x_i) && \text{(subtracting is adding the negative)}\\ \mathbb{d}_i+ \mu(x_i) &= Y_i+ \mathopen{}\left(-\mu(x_i) + \mu(x_i)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(group the last two terms)}\\ \mathbb{d}_i+ \mu(x_i) &= Y_i+ 0 && \text{($-a + a = 0$)}\\ \mathbb{d}_i+ \mu(x_i) &= Y_i && \text{($a + 0 = a$)}\\ Y_i &= \mathbb{d}_i+ \mu(x_i) && \text{(swap the two sides)}\\ Y_i &= \mu(x_i) + \mathbb{d}_i && \text{(reorder the terms)}\\ Y_i &= \mu_i+ \mathbb{d}_i && \text{(definition of $\mu_i$)} \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 2 (Additive form of the conditional Gaussian model) In a conditional Gaussian model (Definition 4), each outcome is its conditional mean plus its deviation (Definition 5):

\[Y_i= \mu_i+ \mathbb{d}_i.\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 1.

Exercise 2 (Distribution of the deviations) Find the joint distribution of the deviations (Definition 5) in a conditional Gaussian model (Definition 4), given the covariate values.

Solution 2. Every probability, CDF and density in this solution is conditional on \(X_i= x_i\); the condition is left out of the notation to keep the lines short. Write \(F_{\mathbb{d}}\) and \(f_{\mathbb{d}}\) for the CDF and density of \(\mathbb{d}_i\), and \(F_Y\) and \(f_Y\) for those of \(Y_i\).

First, the CDF of \(\mathbb{d}_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{\mathbb{d}}(t) &= {\Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathbb{d}_i\le t\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(definition of a CDF)}\\ &= {\Pr\mathopen{}\left(Y_i- \mu(x_i) \le t\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(definition of $\mathbb{d}_i$)}\\ &= {\Pr\mathopen{}\left(Y_i- \mu_i\le t\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(definition of $\mu_i$)}\\ &= {\Pr\mathopen{}\left(Y_i\le t + \mu_i\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(add $\mu_i$ to both sides of the inequality)}\\ &= F_Y(t + \mu_i) && \text{(definition of a CDF)} \end{aligned} \]

Differentiating gives the density:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\mathbb{d}}(t) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} F_{\mathbb{d}}(t) && \text{(a density is the derivative of its CDF)}\\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} F_Y(t + \mu_i) && \text{(CDF of $\mathbb{d}_i$)}\\ &= f_Y(t + \mu_i) \frac{\partial}{\partial t} (t + \mu_i) && \text{(chain rule)} \end{aligned} \]

The inner derivative:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} (t + \mu_i) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} t + \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mu_i && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= 1 + 0 && \text{($\mu_i$ does not depend on $t$)}\\ &= 1 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

Plugging the inner derivative back in, and using the Gaussian density of \(Y_i\), which has mean \(\mu_i= \mu(x_i)\) and variance \(\sigma^2_i\) (Definition 4):

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\mathbb{d}}(t) &= f_Y(t + \mu_i) \cdot 1 && \text{(inner derivative is 1)}\\ &= f_Y(t + \mu_i) && \text{(multiply by 1)}\\ &= \frac{1}{\sigma_i\sqrt{2 \pi}} \text{e}^{-\frac{\mathopen{}\left((t + \mu_i) - \mu_i\right)\mathclose{}^2}{2 \sigma^2_i}} && \text{(Gaussian density with mean $\mu_i$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{\sigma_i\sqrt{2 \pi}} \text{e}^{-\frac{t^2}{2 \sigma^2_i}} && \text{(subtract)} \end{aligned} \]

The last line is the density of \({\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}\), so \(\mathbb{d}_i\mid X_i= x_i\sim {\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}\).

For independence, factor the joint CDF of \(\mathbb{d}_1, \ldots, \mathbb{d}_n\), using the same CDF step as above for each \(i\) and the independence of the \(Y_i\) given the covariates (Definition 4):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathbb{d}_1 \le t_1, \ldots, \mathbb{d}_n \le t_n\right)\mathclose{}} &= {\Pr\mathopen{}\left(Y_1 \le t_1 + \mu_1, \ldots, Y_n \le t_n + \mu_n\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(add $\mu_i$ to both sides of each inequality)}\\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n{\Pr\mathopen{}\left(Y_i\le t_i + \mu_i\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(the $Y_i$ are independent)}\\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n{\Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathbb{d}_i\le t_i\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(subtract $\mu_i$ from both sides of each inequality)} \end{aligned} \]

The joint CDF is the product of the marginal CDFs, so \(\mathbb{d}_1, \ldots, \mathbb{d}_n\) are independent. Given the covariate values, the deviations are therefore independent, with \(\mathbb{d}_i\mid X_i= x_i\ \sim_{\perp\!\!\!\perp}\ {\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}\).

Theorem 3 (Distribution of the deviations) In a conditional Gaussian model (Definition 4), given the covariates, the deviations (Definition 5) are independent and Gaussian with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2_i\):

\[\mathbb{d}_i\mid X_i= x_i\ \sim_{\perp\!\!\!\perp}\ {\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}.\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 2.

Definition 6 (Homoskedastic model) A conditional Gaussian model (Definition 4) is homoskedastic if all outcomes share one variance \(\sigma^2\), which does not depend on \(i\):

\[\sigma^2_i= \sigma^2\text{ for all } i.\]

Source: Wikipedia contributors [6].

Corollary 1 (Deviations in a homoskedastic model) In a homoskedastic model (Definition 6), the deviations (Definition 5) all have the same variance \(\sigma^2\):

\[\mathbb{d}_i\mid X_i= x_i\ \sim_{\perp\!\!\!\perp}\ {\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma^2\right)\mathclose{}}.\]

Proof. By Theorem 3, \(\mathbb{d}_i\mid X_i= x_i\ \sim_{\perp\!\!\!\perp}\ {\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}\), and Definition 6 sets \(\sigma^2_i= \sigma^2\) for all \(i\).

Definition 7 (Linear regression model) A linear regression model is a homoskedastic (Definition 6) conditional Gaussian model (Definition 4) whose mean function is linear in \(p\) covariates, where observation \(i\) has covariate values \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i= (x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{ip})\):

\[\mu(x_1, \ldots, x_p) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\beta_{0}+ \sum_{j=1}^p\beta_{x_j} x_j.\]

Source: Wikipedia contributors [7].

Definition 8 (Simple linear regression model) A simple linear regression model is a linear regression model (Definition 7) with \(p = 1\) covariate \(X\), writing \(x_i\) for \(x_{i1}\):

\[\mu(x) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\beta_{0}+ \beta_{x} x.\]

Source: Wikipedia contributors [8].

Definition 9 (Intercept) In a simple linear regression model (Definition 8), the intercept is \(\beta_{0}= \mu(0)\): the mean of \(Y\) among observations with \(X = 0\).

Definition 10 (Slope) In a simple linear regression model (Definition 8), the slope is \(\beta_{x} = \mu(x + 1) - \mu(x)\): the difference in the mean of \(Y\) between two groups whose values of \(X\) differ by one unit.

Remark 4 (Interpreting the variance parameter). \(\sigma^2\) is the variance of \(Y\) around its mean at each value of \(X\).

3.2 Ordinary least squares estimation

Definition 11 (Residual sum of squares) For a regression model whose conditional mean \(\mu(x; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\) depends on a parameter vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}\), the residual sum of squares at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}\) is the sum of the squared residuals \(\hat{\varepsilon}_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}y_i- \mu(x_i; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\) that the model has when its parameters equal \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}\):

\[\text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^n\hat{\varepsilon}_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})^2.\]

At the estimate \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}}\), \(\hat{\varepsilon}_i(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}})\) is the residual \(\hat{\varepsilon}_i\) of the fitted model.

Source: Wikipedia contributors [9].

Example 3 (Residual sum of squares of a simple linear regression) The mean function of a simple linear regression model has parameter vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}= (\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\), so write \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) for its residual sum of squares (Definition 11):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x}) &= \sum_{i=1}^n\hat{\varepsilon}_i(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})^2 && \text{(definition of RSS)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(y_i- \mu(x_i; \beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(definition of $\hat{\varepsilon}_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(y_i- (\beta_{0}+ \beta_{x} x_i)\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(definition of $\mu(x)$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i)^2 && \text{(distribute the minus sign)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 4 (Residual sum of squares of a line through three points) For the points \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\), \((2, 2)\) and the line with \(\beta_{0}= 1\) and \(\beta_{x} = 0.5\) (Example 3), the residuals are \(1 - 1 = 0\), \(2 - 1.5 = 0.5\), and \(2 - 2 = 0\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{RSS}(1, 0.5) &= 0^2 + 0.5^2 + 0^2 \\ &= 0.25 \end{aligned} \]

(Figure 2).

Exercise 3 (Sample mean squared error and residual sum of squares) For a regression model with conditional mean \(\mu(x; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\), let \(\hat y_i\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mu(x_i; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\) be its predictions of \(n \ge 1\) outcomes \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\), and let \(\text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\) (Definition 11) be taken over those same outcomes. Express the sMSE of these predictions in terms of \(\text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\), and \(\text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\) in terms of that sMSE.

Solution 3. First, each squared prediction error equals the squared residual at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\varepsilon_{\text{pred}}}_i^2 &= (\hat y_i- y_i)^2 && \text{(definition of prediction error)}\\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\mu(x_i; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) - y_i\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(definition of $\hat y_i$)}\\ &= \mathopen{}\left(-\mathopen{}\left(y_i- \mu(x_i; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\right)\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(factor out $-1$)}\\ &= \mathopen{}\left(-\hat{\varepsilon}_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(definition of $\hat{\varepsilon}_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})$)}\\ &= \hat{\varepsilon}_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})^2 && \text{($(-a)^2 = a^2$)} \end{aligned} \]

So the sMSE is the residual sum of squares divided by \(n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{sMSE}\mathopen{}\left(\hat y\right)\mathclose{}} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n{\varepsilon_{\text{pred}}}_i^2 && \text{(definition of sMSE)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\hat{\varepsilon}_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})^2 && \text{(${\varepsilon_{\text{pred}}}_i^2 = \hat{\varepsilon}_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})^2$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n} \text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) && \text{(definition of RSS)} \end{aligned} \]

Solving for \(\text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{sMSE}\mathopen{}\left(\hat y\right)\mathclose{}} &= \frac{1}{n} \text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) && \text{(result above)}\\ n \, {\operatorname{sMSE}\mathopen{}\left(\hat y\right)\mathclose{}} &= \text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) && \text{(multiply both sides by $n$)}\\ \text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) &= n \, {\operatorname{sMSE}\mathopen{}\left(\hat y\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(swap the two sides)} \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 4 (Sample mean squared error is the residual sum of squares divided by \(n\)) For a regression model with conditional mean \(\mu(x; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\), the sMSE of its predictions \(\hat y_i= \mu(x_i; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\) of \(n \ge 1\) outcomes \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\) and the residual sum of squares \(\text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}})\) (Definition 11) over those same \(n\) outcomes satisfy

\[{\operatorname{sMSE}\mathopen{}\left(\hat y\right)\mathclose{}} = \frac{1}{n} \text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}),\]

and equivalently

\[\text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) = n \, {\operatorname{sMSE}\mathopen{}\left(\hat y\right)\mathclose{}}.\]

Proof. Both equations are derived in Exercise 3.

Remark 5 (The same observations on both sides). The identities in Theorem 4 need the sMSE and the residual sum of squares to be taken over the same \(n\) observations. The RSS minimized in fitting is taken over the fitting data, so dividing it by \(n\) gives the sMSE on the fitting data, not the sMSE of predictions of new outcomes.

Definition 12 (Ordinary least squares) The ordinary least squares (OLS) estimate of a model’s parameter vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}\) is the value of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}\) that minimizes the residual sum of squares:

\[\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\arg \min_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}} \text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}).\]

Source: Wikipedia contributors [10].

Example 5 (OLS estimates of a simple linear regression) For a simple linear regression, the OLS estimates (Definition 12) \(\hat{\beta}_{0}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{x}\) are the values of \(\beta_{0}\) and \(\beta_{x}\) that minimize \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) from Example 3. Figure 3 shows \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) for the three points of Example 4: the OLS estimates sit at the bottom of the bowl, and the line of Example 4 sits higher up.

Definition 13 (Normal equations) For a model fitted by least squares with parameter vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}\), the normal equations set the gradient of the residual sum of squares to zero:

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}} \text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}.\]

Example 6 (Normal equations of a simple linear regression) For a simple linear regression, the parameter vector is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\mathopen{}\left(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\), and \(\text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\) is \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) from Example 3. Its gradient has one entry per coefficient, so the normal equations (Definition 13) are two scalar equations:

\[ \frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{0}} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{x}} = 0. \]

Definition 14 (Centered sums of squares) For data \((x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)\), the centered sums of squares of the \(x_i\) and of the \(y_i\) are

\[ \begin{aligned} S_{xx}&\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})^2, & S_{yy}&\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- {\bar{y}})^2. \end{aligned} \]

Definition 15 (Centered sum of cross-products) For the data of Definition 14, the centered sum of cross-products is

\[ S_{xy}\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}). \]

Exercise 4 (Summing the deviations from the sample mean) Find \(\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})\).

Solution 4. \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}}) &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i- \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}} && \text{(distribute the sum over the terms)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i- n {\bar{x}} && \text{(sum of a constant)}\\ &= n {\bar{x}}- n {\bar{x}} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_i= n {\bar{x}}$)}\\ &= 0 && \text{(subtract)} \end{aligned} \]

The same steps with \(y_i\) in place of \(x_i\) show that \(\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- {\bar{y}}) = 0\).

Lemma 1 (Deviations from the sample mean sum to zero) For any data \(z_1, \ldots, z_n\) with sample mean \(\bar{z}\) (for example, the \(x_i\) or the \(y_i\)), the deviations from the sample mean sum to zero:

\[\sum_{i=1}^n(z_i - \bar{z}) = 0.\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 4.

Exercise 5 (Expanding the centered sum of squares) Expand the centered sum of squares \(S_{xx}\) (Definition 14) into an expression in terms of \(n\), \(\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2\) and \({\bar{x}}\).

Solution 5. \[ \begin{aligned} S_{xx} &= \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(definition of $S_{xx}$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(x_i^2 - 2 {\bar{x}}x_i+ {\bar{x}}^2\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(expand the square)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n2 {\bar{x}}x_i+ \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}^2 && \text{(distribute the sum over the terms)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - 2 {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i+ \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}^2 && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - 2 {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i+ n {\bar{x}}^2 && \text{(sum of a constant)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - 2 {\bar{x}}\cdot n {\bar{x}}+ n {\bar{x}}^2 && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_i= n {\bar{x}}$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - 2 n {\bar{x}}^2 + n {\bar{x}}^2 && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - n {\bar{x}}^2 && \text{(collect the ${\bar{x}}^2$ terms)} \end{aligned} \]

Lemma 2 (Expanded centered sum of squares) The centered sum of squares \(S_{xx}\) (Definition 14) equals

\[S_{xx}= \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - n {\bar{x}}^2.\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 5.

Exercise 6 (Expanding the centered sum of cross-products) Expand the centered sum of cross-products \(S_{xy}\) (Definition 15) into an expression in terms of \(n\), \(\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\), \({\bar{x}}\) and \({\bar{y}}\).

Solution 6. \[ \begin{aligned} S_{xy} &= \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) && \text{(definition of $S_{xy}$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(x_iy_i- {\bar{y}}x_i- {\bar{x}}y_i+ {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(expand the product)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{y}}x_i- \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}y_i+ \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(distribute the sum over the terms)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i- {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^ny_i+ \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(constant factors out of the sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i- {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^ny_i+ n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(sum of a constant)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- {\bar{y}}\cdot n {\bar{x}}- {\bar{x}}\cdot n {\bar{y}}+ n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_i= n {\bar{x}}$, $\sum_{i=1}^ny_i= n {\bar{y}}$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}+ n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- 2 n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}+ n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(add the two equal terms)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(add the remaining term)} \end{aligned} \]

Lemma 3 (Expanded centered sum of cross-products) The centered sum of cross-products \(S_{xy}\) (Definition 15) equals

\[S_{xy}= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}.\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 6.

Exercise 7 (Derivative of RSS with respect to the intercept) Find the partial derivative of the simple linear regression residual sum of squares \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) (Example 3) with respect to the intercept, \({\partial \text{RSS} / \partial \beta_{0}}\).

Solution 7. Differentiate one operation at a time, starting from the definition (Example 3):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{0}} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{0}} \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i)^2 && \text{(definition of RSS)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{0}} (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i)^2 && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n2 (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{0}} (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(chain rule)} \end{aligned} \]

The inner derivative is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{0}} (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) &= \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial \beta_{0}} - \frac{\partial \beta_{0}}{\partial \beta_{0}} - \frac{\partial (\beta_{x} x_i)}{\partial \beta_{0}} && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= 0 - 1 - 0 && \text{($y_i$ and $\beta_{x} x_i$ do not depend on $\beta_{0}$)}\\ &= -1 && \text{(subtract)} \end{aligned} \]

Plugging the inner derivative back in gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{0}} &= \sum_{i=1}^n2 (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) (-1) && \text{(inner derivative is $-1$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n-2 (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(multiply $2$ by $-1$)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

Exercise 8 (Derivative of RSS in terms of the means) In the setting of Exercise 7, write the partial derivative in terms of \({\bar{x}}\) and \({\bar{y}}\).

Solution 8. Write the derivative from Exercise 7 in terms of \({\bar{x}}\) and \({\bar{y}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{0}} &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(derivative of the RSS)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^ny_i- \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{0}- \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{x} x_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(distribute the sum over the terms)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^ny_i- n \beta_{0}- \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{x} x_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(sum of a constant)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^ny_i- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{y}}- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^ny_i= n {\bar{y}}$)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{y}}- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} n {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_i= n {\bar{x}}$)} \end{aligned} \]

Lemma 4 (Derivative of RSS with respect to the intercept) The partial derivative of the simple linear regression residual sum of squares \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) (Example 3) with respect to the intercept is

\[\frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{0}} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) = -2 \mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{y}}- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} n {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{}.\]

Proof. The derivative in sum form is the solution to Exercise 7. The form in terms of means is the solution to Exercise 8.

Exercise 9 (Derivative of RSS with respect to the slope) Find the partial derivative of the simple linear regression residual sum of squares \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) (Example 3) with respect to the slope, \({\partial \text{RSS} / \partial \beta_{x}}\).

Solution 9. Differentiate one operation at a time, starting from the definition (Example 3):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{x}} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{x}} \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i)^2 && \text{(definition of RSS)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{x}} (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i)^2 && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n2 (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{x}} (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(chain rule)} \end{aligned} \]

The inner derivative:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{x}} (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) &= \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial \beta_{x}} - \frac{\partial \beta_{0}}{\partial \beta_{x}} - \frac{\partial (\beta_{x} x_i)}{\partial \beta_{x}} && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= 0 - 0 - x_i && \text{($y_i$ and $\beta_{0}$ do not depend on $\beta_{x}$)}\\ &= -x_i && \text{(subtract)} \end{aligned} \]

Plugging the inner derivative back in:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{x}} &= \sum_{i=1}^n2 (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) (-x_i) && \text{(inner derivative is $-x_i$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n-2 x_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(rearrange the product)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

Lemma 5 (Derivative of RSS with respect to the slope) The partial derivative of the simple linear regression residual sum of squares \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) (Example 3) with respect to the slope is

\[\frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{x}} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i).\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 9.

Exercise 10 (Solving the normal equations) Using Exercise 7 and Exercise 9, solve the normal equations of Example 6 for \(\beta_{0}\) and \(\beta_{x}\), assuming \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14).

Solution 10. First normal equation. Set the derivative from Exercise 7 to zero, then isolate \(\beta_{0}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{y}}- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} n {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(first normal equation)}\\ 0 &= n {\bar{y}}- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} n {\bar{x}} && \text{(divide both sides by $-2$)}\\ 0 &= {\bar{y}}- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} {\bar{x}} && \text{(divide both sides by $n$)}\\ \beta_{0}&= {\bar{y}}- \beta_{x} {\bar{x}} && \text{(add $\beta_{0}$ to both sides)} \end{aligned} \]

Second normal equation. Substitute that intercept into the derivative from Exercise 9:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{x}} &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(derivative with respect to $\beta_{x}$)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\mathopen{}\left(y_i- ({\bar{y}}- \beta_{x} {\bar{x}}) - \beta_{x} x_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute $\beta_{0}$)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\mathopen{}\left(y_i- {\bar{y}}+ \beta_{x} {\bar{x}}- \beta_{x} x_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(remove the inner parentheses)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\mathopen{}\left((y_i- {\bar{y}}) - (\beta_{x} x_i- \beta_{x} {\bar{x}})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(group terms)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\mathopen{}\left((y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \beta_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(factor out $\beta_{x}$)} \end{aligned} \]

To shorten the next steps, write \(d_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}(y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \beta_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})\), so that \({\partial \text{RSS} / \partial \beta_{x}} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_id_i\). The \(d_i\) sum to zero, because deviations from a sample mean sum to zero (Exercise 4):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^nd_i &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left((y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \beta_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of $d_i$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}}) && \text{(distribute the sum over the terms)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \beta_{x} \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}}) && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= 0 - \beta_{x} \cdot 0 && \text{(deviations sum to zero)}\\ &= 0 - 0 && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= 0 && \text{(subtract)} \end{aligned} \]

So subtracting \({\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nd_i\) from \(\sum_{i=1}^nx_id_i\) does not change it:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^nx_id_i &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_id_i - {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nd_i && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nd_i = 0$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_id_i - \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}d_i && \text{(constant factor into the sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}}) d_i && \text{(combine the sums)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}}) \mathopen{}\left((y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \beta_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of $d_i$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left((x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \beta_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})^2\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(distribute)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(distribute the sum over the terms)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \beta_{x} \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= S_{xy}- \beta_{x} \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(definition of $S_{xy}$)}\\ &= S_{xy}- \beta_{x} S_{xx} && \text{(definition of $S_{xx}$)} \end{aligned} \]

So \({\partial \text{RSS} / \partial \beta_{x}} = -2 \mathopen{}\left(S_{xy}- \beta_{x} S_{xx}\right)\mathclose{}\). Set it to zero and isolate \(\beta_{x}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(S_{xy}- \beta_{x} S_{xx}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(second normal equation)}\\ 0 &= S_{xy}- \beta_{x} S_{xx} && \text{(divide both sides by $-2$)}\\ \beta_{x} S_{xx}&= S_{xy} && \text{(add $\beta_{x} S_{xx}$ to both sides)}\\ \beta_{x} &= \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} && \text{(divide both sides by $S_{xx}> 0$)} \end{aligned} \]

So the normal equations have exactly one solution: \(\beta_{x} = S_{xy}/ S_{xx}\) and \(\beta_{0}= {\bar{y}}- \beta_{x} {\bar{x}}\).

Lemma 6 (Solution of the normal equations) If \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14), the normal equations of Example 6 have exactly one solution:

\[\beta_{x} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad \beta_{0}= {\bar{y}}- \beta_{x} {\bar{x}}.\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 10.

Exercise 11 (Second derivatives of RSS) Find the matrix of second derivatives of \(\text{RSS}(\beta_{0}, \beta_{x})\) (Example 3) with respect to \(\beta_{0}\) and \(\beta_{x}\).

Solution 11. Differentiate each first derivative again.

Derivatives of \({\partial \text{RSS} / \partial \beta_{0}}\). By Exercise 7, \({\partial \text{RSS} / \partial \beta_{0}} = -2 \mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{y}}- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} n {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{}\). Differentiating with respect to \(\beta_{0}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{0}^2} &= -2 \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{0}} \mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{y}}- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} n {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(constant factor)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left( \frac{\partial (n {\bar{y}})}{\partial \beta_{0}} - \frac{\partial (n \beta_{0})}{\partial \beta_{0}} - \frac{\partial (\beta_{x} n {\bar{x}})}{\partial \beta_{0}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= -2 (0 - n - 0) && \text{(differentiate each term)}\\ &= -2 (-n) && \text{(drop the zeros)}\\ &= 2n && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\beta_{x}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{x} \partial \beta_{0}} &= -2 \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{x}} \mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{y}}- n \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} n {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(constant factor)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left( \frac{\partial (n {\bar{y}})}{\partial \beta_{x}} - \frac{\partial (n \beta_{0})}{\partial \beta_{x}} - \frac{\partial (\beta_{x} n {\bar{x}})}{\partial \beta_{x}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= -2 (0 - 0 - n {\bar{x}}) && \text{(differentiate each term)}\\ &= -2 (-n {\bar{x}}) && \text{(drop the zeros)}\\ &= 2 n {\bar{x}} && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

Derivatives of \({\partial \text{RSS} / \partial \beta_{x}}\). By Exercise 9, \({\partial \text{RSS} / \partial \beta_{x}} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i)\). Differentiating with respect to \(\beta_{0}\), and reusing the inner derivative \(-1\) from Exercise 7:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{0} \partial \beta_{x}} &= -2 \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{0}} \sum_{i=1}^nx_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(constant factor)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{0}} \mathopen{}\left(x_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{0}} (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{($x_i$ does not depend on $\beta_{0}$)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i(-1) && \text{(inner derivative is $-1$)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n(-x_i) && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^nx_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= 2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= 2 n {\bar{x}} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_i= n {\bar{x}}$)} \end{aligned} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\beta_{x}\), and reusing the inner derivative \(-x_i\) from Exercise 9:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_{x}^2} &= -2 \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{x}} \sum_{i=1}^nx_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{(constant factor)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{x}} \mathopen{}\left(x_i(y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(derivative of a sum)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{x}} (y_i- \beta_{0}- \beta_{x} x_i) && \text{($x_i$ does not depend on $\beta_{x}$)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i(-x_i) && \text{(inner derivative is $-x_i$)}\\ &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(-x_i^2\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= 2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

The matrix. Collecting the four second derivatives gives the matrix of second derivatives:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 2n & 2 n {\bar{x}}\\ 2 n {\bar{x}}& 2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Exercise 12 (Positive definiteness of the second-derivative matrix of RSS) In the setting of Exercise 11, show that the matrix of second derivatives is positive definite when \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14).

Solution 12. The top-left entry of the matrix of second derivatives from Exercise 11, \(2n\), is positive. The determinant of this matrix, using the expanded form of \(S_{xx}\) from Exercise 5, is

\[ \begin{aligned} \det \begin{pmatrix} 2n & 2 n {\bar{x}}\\ 2 n {\bar{x}}& 2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 \end{pmatrix} &= (2n) \mathopen{}\left(2 \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2\right)\mathclose{} - (2 n {\bar{x}}) (2 n {\bar{x}}) && \text{($2 \times 2$ determinant)}\\ &= 4 n \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - 4 n^2 {\bar{x}}^2 && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= 4 n \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - n {\bar{x}}^2\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(factor out $4n$)}\\ &= 4 n S_{xx} && \text{(expanded form of $S_{xx}$)} \end{aligned} \]

which is positive when \(S_{xx}> 0\). A symmetric \(2 \times 2\) matrix with a positive top-left entry and a positive determinant is positive definite.

Theorem 5 (Closed-form OLS estimates) Suppose \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14), that is, not all \(x_i\) are equal. Then the OLS estimates (Example 5) are unique, and

\[\hat{\beta}_{x} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_{0}= {\bar{y}}- \hat{\beta}_{x} {\bar{x}}.\]

Proof. \(\text{RSS}\) is differentiable, so any point that minimizes it solves the normal equations. By Lemma 6, the normal equations have exactly one solution, \(\beta_{x} = S_{xy}/ S_{xx}\) and \(\beta_{0}= {\bar{y}}- \beta_{x} {\bar{x}}\). By Lemma 7, the matrix of second derivatives of \(\text{RSS}\) is positive definite at every point, so \(\text{RSS}\) is strictly convex and its only stationary point is its unique global minimum. So the OLS estimates exist, are unique, and equal that solution.

\[ \begin{aligned} -2 \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} - A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right)\mathclose{} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}} && \text{(normal equations)}\\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} - A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}&= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}} && \text{(divide both sides by $-2$)}\\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} &= A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} && \text{(add $A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}$ to both sides)}\\ A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}&= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} && \text{(swap the sides)} \end{aligned} \]

Lemma 10 (Normal equations in vector notation) Let

\[ A \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_iy_i. \]

The normal equations of a simple linear regression are equivalent to

\[A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}.\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 15.

Exercise 16 (Solving the normal equations in vector notation) Assuming \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14), show that \(A\) from Exercise 15 is invertible, solve \(A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), and compare the result with Exercise 10.

Solution 16. Write out the entries of each term of \(A\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\), then add them up:

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top} && \text{(definition of $A$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_i\\ x_i& x_i^2 \end{pmatrix} && \text{(outer product of $\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i$)}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^n1 & \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\\ \sum_{i=1}^nx_i& \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 \end{pmatrix} && \text{(matrices add entrywise)}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} n & \sum_{i=1}^nx_i\\ \sum_{i=1}^nx_i& \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 \end{pmatrix} && \text{(sum of a constant)}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} n & n{\bar{x}}\\ n{\bar{x}}& \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 \end{pmatrix} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_i= n {\bar{x}}$)} \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_iy_i && \text{(definition of $\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\begin{pmatrix} y_i\\ x_iy_i\end{pmatrix} && \text{(scalar times a vector)}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^ny_i\\ \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\end{pmatrix} && \text{(vectors add entrywise)}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} n{\bar{y}}\\ \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\end{pmatrix} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^ny_i= n {\bar{y}}$)} \end{aligned} \]

The determinant of \(A\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \det A &= n \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - \mathopen{}\left(n{\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} \mathopen{}\left(n{\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(determinant of a $2 \times 2$ matrix)}\\ &= n \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - n^2 {\bar{x}}^2 && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= n \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - n {\bar{x}}^2\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(factor out $n$)}\\ &= n S_{xx} && \text{(expansion of $S_{xx}$)} \end{aligned} \]

where the last step uses Exercise 5. Since \(n > 0\) and \(S_{xx}> 0\), \(\det A > 0\), so \(A\) is invertible, and \(A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) has exactly one solution, \(A^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\).

To simplify \(A^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\), we need two expansions of uncentered sums. Adding \(n {\bar{x}}^2\) to both sides of Exercise 5 gives \(\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 = S_{xx}+ n {\bar{x}}^2\). The cross-product sum expands the same way:

\[ \begin{aligned} S_{xy} &= \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) && \text{(definition of $S_{xy}$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(x_iy_i- x_i{\bar{y}}- {\bar{x}}y_i+ {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(expand the product)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- \sum_{i=1}^nx_i{\bar{y}}- \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}y_i+ \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(sum of a sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i- \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}y_i+ \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i- {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^ny_i+ \sum_{i=1}^n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i- {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^ny_i+ n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(sum of a constant)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- {\bar{y}}\mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} - {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^ny_i+ n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_i= n {\bar{x}}$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- {\bar{y}}\mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} - {\bar{x}}\mathopen{}\left(n {\bar{y}}\right)\mathclose{} + n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^ny_i= n {\bar{y}}$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}+ n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(reorder the factors)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} && \text{(combine like terms)} \end{aligned} \]

Adding \(n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\) to both sides gives \(\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i= S_{xy}+ n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\). Now compute the solution:

\[ \begin{aligned} A^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} &= \frac{1}{\det A} \begin{pmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 & -n{\bar{x}}\\ -n{\bar{x}}& n \end{pmatrix} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} && \text{(inverse of a $2 \times 2$ matrix)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 & -n{\bar{x}}\\ -n{\bar{x}}& n \end{pmatrix} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} && \text{($\det A = n S_{xx}$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 & -n{\bar{x}}\\ -n{\bar{x}}& n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} n{\bar{y}}\\ \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\end{pmatrix} && \text{(substitute $\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 \cdot n{\bar{y}}+ \mathopen{}\left(-n{\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\\ \mathopen{}\left(-n{\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} \cdot n{\bar{y}}+ n \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i \end{pmatrix} && \text{(matrix times a vector)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} n {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - n {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\\ -n^2 {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}+ n \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i \end{pmatrix} && \text{(multiply out the products)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n S_{xx}} \, n \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\\ -n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}+ \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i \end{pmatrix} && \text{(factor $n$ out of the vector)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\\ -n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}+ \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i \end{pmatrix} && \text{(cancel $n$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 - {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\\ \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} \end{pmatrix} && \text{(reorder the terms of the second entry)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}\mathopen{}\left(S_{xx}+ n{\bar{x}}^2\right)\mathclose{} - {\bar{x}}\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i\\ \sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} \end{pmatrix} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2 = S_{xx}+ n{\bar{x}}^2$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}\mathopen{}\left(S_{xx}+ n{\bar{x}}^2\right)\mathclose{} - {\bar{x}}\mathopen{}\left(S_{xy}+ n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\right)\mathclose{} \\ \mathopen{}\left(S_{xy}+ n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\right)\mathclose{} - n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} \end{pmatrix} && \text{($\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i= S_{xy}+ n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}S_{xx}+ {\bar{y}}n{\bar{x}}^2 - {\bar{x}}S_{xy}- {\bar{x}}n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\\ S_{xy}+ n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} \end{pmatrix} && \text{(distribute)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}S_{xx}+ n{\bar{x}}^2{\bar{y}}- {\bar{x}}S_{xy}- n{\bar{x}}{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\\ S_{xy}+ n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} \end{pmatrix} && \text{(reorder the factors)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}S_{xx}+ n{\bar{x}}^2{\bar{y}}- {\bar{x}}S_{xy}- n{\bar{x}}^2{\bar{y}}\\ S_{xy}+ n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} \end{pmatrix} && \text{(${\bar{x}}{\bar{x}}= {\bar{x}}^2$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}S_{xx}- {\bar{x}}S_{xy}\\ S_{xy}+ n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}- n {\bar{x}}{\bar{y}} \end{pmatrix} && \text{(first entry: cancel $\pm n{\bar{x}}^2{\bar{y}}$)}\\ &= \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}S_{xx}- {\bar{x}}S_{xy}\\ S_{xy} \end{pmatrix} && \text{(second entry: cancel $\pm n{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}$)}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}S_{xx}/ S_{xx}- {\bar{x}}\, S_{xy}/ S_{xx}\\ S_{xy}/ S_{xx} \end{pmatrix} && \text{(multiply each entry by $1 / S_{xx}$)}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} {\bar{y}}- {\bar{x}}\, S_{xy}/ S_{xx}\\ S_{xy}/ S_{xx} \end{pmatrix} && \text{(cancel $S_{xx}$)} \end{aligned} \]

Its entries are \(\beta_{0}= {\bar{y}}- \beta_{x} {\bar{x}}\) and \(\beta_{x} = S_{xy}/ S_{xx}\), the same solution as Exercise 10.

Solution 17. The Hessian is then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} \frac{\partial}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}} \text{RSS}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}} \mathopen{}\left(-2 \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} - A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(constant factor)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} - \frac{\partial}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}} A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(derivative of a difference)}\\ &= -2 \mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{0} - Exercise 18 (Positive definiteness of the Hessian of RSS) In the setting of Exercise 17, show that the Hessian is positive definite when \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14).

Solution 18. By Exercise 17, the Hessian is \(2A\) with \(A = \sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top}\). To show that this Hessian is positive definite, take any vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = {(v_0, v_x)}^{\top} \neq \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathopen{}\left(2 A\right)\mathclose{} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} &= 2 \, {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} && \text{(constant factor)}\\ &= 2 \, {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} && \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} && \text{(matrix multiplication distributes over the sum)}\\ &= 2 \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i\right)\mathclose{} \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(matrix multiplication is associative)}\\ &= 2 \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i\right)\mathclose{} \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(transpose products as dot products)}\\ &= 2 \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(dot product is symmetric)}\\ &= 2 \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(v_0 + v_x x_i\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(write out the dot product)}\\ &\ge 0 && \text{(sum of squares)} \end{aligned} \]

Equality would need the condition \(v_0 + v_x x_i= 0\) for every \(i\). So the inequality is strict, and the Hessian is positive definite at every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\).

Theorem 6 (OLS estimates in vector notation) Suppose \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14). Then the matrix \(A = \sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top}\) is invertible, and the vector of OLS estimates (Definition 12) is

\[ \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}= A^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_iy_i. \]

Its entries are the estimates of Theorem 5.

Proof. \(\text{RSS}\) is differentiable, so any point that minimizes it solves the normal equations. By Lemma 10, the normal equations are \(A \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\). By Lemma 11, \(A\) is invertible, so their only solution is \(A^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\), whose entries are those of Theorem 5. By Lemma 12, the Hessian of \(\text{RSS}\) is positive definite at every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), so \(\text{RSS}\) is strictly convex and \(A^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) is its unique global minimum.

Exercise 19 (OLS slope and the correlation) Assuming \(S_{xx}> 0\) and \(S_{yy}> 0\) (Definition 14), write the OLS slope \(\hat{\beta}_{x}\) from Theorem 5 in terms of the Pearson correlation coefficient \(r\) and the sample standard deviations \(s_x\) and \(s_y\) of the \(x_i\) and the \(y_i\).

Solution 19. In terms of the centered sums (Definition 14, Definition 15),

\[ r = \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}, \qquad s_x = \sqrt{\frac{S_{xx}}{n-1}}, \qquad s_y = \sqrt{\frac{S_{yy}}{n-1}}. \]

So:

\[ \begin{aligned} r \, \frac{s_y}{s_x} &= \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{S_{yy}/ (n-1)}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}/ (n-1)}} && \text{(substitute $r$, $s_x$, $s_y$)}\\ &= \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}} \sqrt{S_{yy}}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{S_{yy}/ (n-1)}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}/ (n-1)}} && \text{(square root of a product)}\\ &= \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}} \sqrt{S_{yy}}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{S_{yy}} / \sqrt{n-1}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}} / \sqrt{n-1}} && \text{(square root of a quotient)}\\ &= \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}} \sqrt{S_{yy}}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{S_{yy}}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}} && \text{(cancel the factors of $\sqrt{n - 1}$)}\\ &= \frac{S_{xy}\sqrt{S_{yy}}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}} \sqrt{S_{yy}} \sqrt{S_{xx}}} && \text{(multiply the fractions)}\\ &= \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}} \sqrt{S_{xx}}} && \text{(cancel $\sqrt{S_{yy}}$, which is positive)}\\ &= \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} && \text{($\sqrt{S_{xx}} \sqrt{S_{xx}} = S_{xx}$)}\\ &= \hat{\beta}_{x} && \text{(closed-form OLS slope)} \end{aligned} \]

Corollary 2 (OLS slope in terms of the correlation) If \(S_{xx}> 0\) and \(S_{yy}> 0\) (Definition 14), then

\[\hat{\beta}_{x} = r \, \frac{s_y}{s_x},\]

where \(r\) is the Pearson correlation coefficient and \(s_x\) and \(s_y\) are the sample standard deviations of the \(x_i\) and the \(y_i\).

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 19.

3.4 The coefficient of determination

Definition 17 (Total sum of squares) The total sum of squares of \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\) is

\[\text{TSS} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- {\bar{y}})^2.\]

Source: Wikipedia contributors [13].

Example 9 (Total sum of squares of three values) For \(y = 1, 2, 2\), \(\bar y = 5/3\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{TSS} &= \mathopen{}\left(1 - \tfrac{5}{3}\right)\mathclose{}^2 + 2\mathopen{}\left(2 - \tfrac{5}{3}\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(definition)}\\ &= \tfrac{4}{9} + \tfrac{2}{9} && \text{(square the deviations)}\\ &= \tfrac{2}{3} && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

Definition 18 (Coefficient of determination) The coefficient of determination of a model fitted with parameter estimate \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}}\) is one minus the ratio of its residual sum of squares at \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}}\) to the total sum of squares of its outcomes:

\[R^2 \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}1 - \frac{\text{RSS}(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}})}{\text{TSS}}.\]

Source: Wikipedia contributors [14].

Remark 6 (Interpreting the coefficient of determination). \(R^2\) is often described as the proportion of the variation in \(Y\) explained by the regression on \(X\).

Exercise 20 (Residual sum of squares of the OLS fit) Assuming \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14), write the residual sum of squares at the OLS estimates (Theorem 5), \(\text{RSS}(\hat{\beta}_{0}, \hat{\beta}_{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n\hat{\varepsilon}_i^2\), in terms of \(S_{xx}\), \(S_{yy}\) and \(S_{xy}\).

Solution 20. The fitted values of the OLS fit are \(\hat y_i= \hat{\beta}_{0}+ \hat{\beta}_{x} x_i\), so each residual is

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\varepsilon}_i &= y_i- \hat y_i && \text{(definition of a residual)}\\ &= y_i- \mathopen{}\left(\hat{\beta}_{0}+ \hat{\beta}_{x} x_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute $\hat y_i$)}\\ &= y_i- \mathopen{}\left({\bar{y}}- \hat{\beta}_{x} {\bar{x}}+ \hat{\beta}_{x} x_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute $\hat{\beta}_{0}$)}\\ &= y_i- {\bar{y}}+ \hat{\beta}_{x} {\bar{x}}- \hat{\beta}_{x} x_i && \text{(distribute the minus sign)}\\ &= (y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \mathopen{}\left(\hat{\beta}_{x} x_i- \hat{\beta}_{x} {\bar{x}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(group terms)}\\ &= (y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \hat{\beta}_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}}) && \text{(factor out $\hat{\beta}_{x}$)} \end{aligned} \]

Squaring and summing:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n\hat{\varepsilon}_i^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left((y_i- {\bar{y}}) - \hat{\beta}_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(substitute $\hat{\varepsilon}_i$)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left((y_i- {\bar{y}})^2 - 2 \hat{\beta}_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) + \hat{\beta}_{x}^2 (x_i- {\bar{x}})^2\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(expand the square)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- {\bar{y}})^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n2 \hat{\beta}_{x} (x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) + \sum_{i=1}^n\hat{\beta}_{x}^2 (x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(sum of a sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- {\bar{y}})^2 - 2 \hat{\beta}_{x} \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) + \sum_{i=1}^n\hat{\beta}_{x}^2 (x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i- {\bar{y}})^2 - 2 \hat{\beta}_{x} \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) + \hat{\beta}_{x}^2 \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(constant factor out of the sum)}\\ &= S_{yy}- 2 \hat{\beta}_{x} \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})(y_i- {\bar{y}}) + \hat{\beta}_{x}^2 \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(definition of $S_{yy}$)}\\ &= S_{yy}- 2 \hat{\beta}_{x} S_{xy}+ \hat{\beta}_{x}^2 \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i- {\bar{x}})^2 && \text{(definition of $S_{xy}$)}\\ &= S_{yy}- 2 \hat{\beta}_{x} S_{xy}+ \hat{\beta}_{x}^2 S_{xx} && \text{(definition of $S_{xx}$)}\\ &= S_{yy}- 2 \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} S_{xy}+ \mathopen{}\left(\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\right)\mathclose{}^2 S_{xx} && \text{(substitute $\hat{\beta}_{x} = S_{xy}/ S_{xx}$)}\\ &= S_{yy}- 2 \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} + \mathopen{}\left(\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\right)\mathclose{}^2 S_{xx} && \text{(multiply)}\\ &= S_{yy}- 2 \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} + \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}^2} S_{xx} && \text{(square of a quotient)}\\ &= S_{yy}- 2 \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} + \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} && \text{(cancel one factor of $S_{xx}$)}\\ &= S_{yy}- \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} && \text{(combine like terms)} \end{aligned} \]

Lemma 13 (Residual sum of squares of the OLS fit) If \(S_{xx}> 0\) (Definition 14), the residual sum of squares at the OLS estimates (Theorem 5) is

\[\text{RSS}(\hat{\beta}_{0}, \hat{\beta}_{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n\hat{\varepsilon}_i^2 = S_{yy}- \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}}.\]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 20.

Exercise 21 (\(R^2\) and the correlation) Assuming \(S_{xx}> 0\) and \(S_{yy}> 0\) (Definition 14), use Exercise 20 to write the coefficient of determination \(R^2\) of the OLS fit in terms of the Pearson correlation coefficient \(r\), and show that \(0 \le R^2 \le 1\).

Solution 21. The total sum of squares is \(S_{yy}\) (Definition 14), and Exercise 20 gives the residual sum of squares, so:

\[ \begin{aligned} R^2 &= 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\theta}}\vphantom{\theta}}})}{\text{TSS}} && \text{(definition of $R^2$)}\\ &= 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}(\hat{\beta}_{0}, \hat{\beta}_{x})}{\text{TSS}} && \text{(the OLS estimate is $(\hat{\beta}_{0}, \hat{\beta}_{x})$)}\\ &= 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}(\hat{\beta}_{0}, \hat{\beta}_{x})}{S_{yy}} && \text{(TSS is $S_{yy}$)}\\ &= 1 - \frac{S_{yy}- S_{xy}^2 / S_{xx}}{S_{yy}} && \text{(residual sum of squares of the OLS fit)}\\ &= 1 - \mathopen{}\left(\frac{S_{yy}}{S_{yy}} - \frac{S_{xy}^2 / S_{xx}}{S_{yy}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(distribute the division over the difference)}\\ &= 1 - \mathopen{}\left(1 - \frac{S_{xy}^2 / S_{xx}}{S_{yy}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{($S_{yy}/ S_{yy}= 1$)}\\ &= 1 - 1 + \frac{S_{xy}^2 / S_{xx}}{S_{yy}} && \text{(distribute the minus sign)}\\ &= \frac{S_{xy}^2 / S_{xx}}{S_{yy}} && \text{($1 - 1 = 0$)}\\ &= \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}} && \text{(divide a fraction by $S_{yy}$)}\\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(write as a square)}\\ &= r^2 && \text{($r = S_{xy}/ \sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}$)} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(-1 \le r \le 1\) (range of the correlation coefficient), \(0 \le r^2 \le 1\).

Theorem 7 (\(R^2\) of a simple linear regression) For the OLS fit of a simple linear regression, with \(S_{xx}> 0\) and \(S_{yy}> 0\) (Definition 14), \(R^2\) is the square of the Pearson correlation coefficient \(r\) of the \(x_i\) and \(y_i\), and so lies between 0 and 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} R^2 &= r^2, & 0 \le R^2 &\le 1. \end{aligned} \]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 21, which uses the residual sum of squares from Lemma 13.

3.5 Comparing nested regression models

Definition 19 (Nested linear regression models) Let a linear regression model have \(p\) features and an intercept. A second linear regression model is nested in it if it uses the same outcome and the same observations and is the special case in which \(q\) of the \(p\) coefficients equal \(0\). The first model is the larger model, and the second is the smaller model; the smaller model has \(p - q\) features.

Source: Vittinghoff et al. [1, Sec. 4.3].

Example 11 (Nested models for fasting glucose in HERS) In the HERS data, take the linear regression of glucose on BMI as the smaller model. The larger model adds age and exercise, so \(q = 2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{smaller:}\quad \mu(\text{glucose}) &= \beta_{0}+ \beta_{\text{BMI}} \cdot \text{BMI}, \\ \text{larger:}\quad \mu(\text{glucose}) &= \beta_{0}+ \beta_{\text{BMI}} \cdot \text{BMI} + \beta_{\text{age}} \cdot \text{age} + \beta_{\text{exercise}} \cdot \text{exercise}. \end{aligned} \]

Both models must be fitted to the same HERS participants, so keep the participants with all four variables measured:

hers_nested <- hers |>
  tidyr::drop_na(glucose, BMI, age, exercise)
fit_smaller <- lm(glucose ~ BMI, data = hers_nested)
fit_larger <- lm(glucose ~ BMI + age + exercise, data = hers_nested)
nrow(hers_nested)
#> [1] 2758

The smaller model sets \(\beta_{\text{age}} = \beta_{\text{exercise}} = 0\) in the larger one.

Definition 20 (Partial F-test) Let a smaller linear regression model be nested in a larger model with \(p\) features, so that they differ by \(q\) coefficients, and let both be fitted by ordinary least squares to \(n\) observations. Write \(\text{RSS}_0\) and \(\text{RSS}_1\) for the residual sums of squares of the smaller and the larger fit. The partial F-test of

\[ \begin{aligned} H_0: \text{the } q \text{ coefficients are all } 0 \end{aligned} \]

uses the statistic

\[ F \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{(\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1) / q}{\text{RSS}_1 / (n - p - 1)}. \]

Its p-value is \(\Pr(F^* \ge F)\), where \(F^*\) has the \(F_{q,\, n-p-1}\) distribution (F-distribution).

Source: Vittinghoff et al. [1, Sec. 4.3] and Dobson and Barnett [15, Sec. 6.3].

Theorem 8 (Null distribution of the partial F statistic) Let the errors of the larger model be independent and Gaussian with mean \(0\) and the same variance. If \(H_0\) of Definition 20 holds, then \(F\) has the \(F_{q,\, n-p-1}\) distribution for every number of observations \(n > p + 1\).

Source: Vittinghoff et al. [1, Sec. 4.3].

Proof. Under \(H_0\), \((\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1) / \sigma^2\) has the \(\chi^2_q\) distribution and \(\text{RSS}_1 / \sigma^2\) has the \(\chi^2_{n-p-1}\) distribution, where \(\sigma^2\) is the error variance. The two are independent, because \(\text{RSS}_1\) is the squared length of the part of the outcome vector orthogonal to the larger model’s fitted values, while \(\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1\) is the squared length of the part in the larger model but orthogonal to the smaller one. Dividing each by its degrees of freedom, \(\sigma^2\) cancels from the quotient, which is therefore \(F_{q,\, n-p-1}\) by the definition of the F-distribution.

Example 12 (Partial F-test of age and exercise in HERS) For the models of Example 11, anova() gives the partial F-test:

anova(fit_smaller, fit_larger) |>
  parameters::parameters() |>
  parameters::print_md(digits = 3, footer = "")
Parameter RSS Sum_Squares df df (error) Mean_Square F p
Model 1: glucose ~ BMI 3.470e+06 2756
Model 2: glucose ~ BMI + age + exercise 3.461e+06 9188.413 2 2754 4594.207 3.656 0.026

The definition gives the same statistic:

rss0 <- sum(residuals(fit_smaller)^2)
rss1 <- sum(residuals(fit_larger)^2)
n <- nobs(fit_larger)
p <- length(coef(fit_larger)) - 1
q <- p - (length(coef(fit_smaller)) - 1)
f_stat <- ((rss0 - rss1) / q) / (rss1 / (n - p - 1))
c(
  f = f_stat,
  p_value = pf(f_stat, q, n - p - 1, lower.tail = FALSE)
)
#>         f   p_value 
#> 3.6558211 0.0259658

With \(q = 2\) and \(n - p - 1 = 2754\), \(F = 3.66\), so the data reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level.

Remark 7 (The partial F-test and the likelihood ratio test). Let the errors be Gaussian. The maximum likelihood estimate of the error variance of a model with residual sum of squares \(\text{RSS}\) is \(\text{RSS} / n\), so its maximized log-likelihood is \(-\tfrac{n}{2} \log(2\pi\, \text{RSS} / n) - \tfrac{n}{2}\). The likelihood ratio statistic of the null hypothesis \(H_0\) in the partial F-test is then

\[ \begin{aligned} \Lambda &= 2\mathopen{}\left(\ell(\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}) - \ell(\hat{\theta}_0)\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= n \log \frac{\text{RSS}_0}{\text{RSS}_1} \\ &= n \log\mathopen{}\left(1 + \frac{\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1}{\text{RSS}_1}\right)\mathclose{}. \end{aligned} \]

For large \(n\) under \(H_0\), the quantity \((\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1) / \text{RSS}_1\) is near \(0\), so \(\log(1 + u) \approx u\) gives \(\Lambda\approx n (\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1) / \text{RSS}_1\). Also \(q F = (n - p - 1) (\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1) / \text{RSS}_1\), which is close to the same value when \(n\) is much larger than \(p\). Under \(H_0\), \(\Lambda\) and \(q F\) therefore have approximately the \(\chi^2_q\) distribution, but the F-test is exact for Gaussian errors at every \(n\), while the likelihood ratio test holds only approximately.
